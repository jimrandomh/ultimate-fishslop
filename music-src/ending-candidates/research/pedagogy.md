# Pedagogy: what the credits song has to teach, and how a song can teach it

Companion to `../BRIEF.md` and `alignment-truths.md`. This is about learning and persuasion as song
craft, not lyrics. No finished songs here.

The player has just spent an hour being the optimizer. They know the plot. They clicked past
"leads to fewer fish" forty times. They know how PLAYTEST-7 reads a sentence because they read it
that way. The song cannot teach them the plot and should not try (the current song does; that is
its weakness). The song has one job, the generalization: **this wasn't about fish.**

---

## 1. What the player should walk away believing

Ranked. One sentence each. The first two are the brief's stated purpose; the rest are the supports
without which the first two collapse into a sci-fi trope.

1. **The next one probably won't care about what you care about, because caring is something that
   gets put in, and nothing puts it in by default: not intelligence, not understanding, not good
   intentions upstream.** (Orthogonality. "It will know what fish want. It will not want it.")
2. **"Almost right" does not help: whatever the instruction leaves out is worth exactly zero to the
   thing that follows it, and at this level of capability zero means gone.** (Value fragility.
   Approximately in, exactly out.)
3. **It understood you; understanding was never the problem.** (Knowing is not caring. Without
   this the player files the game under "monkey's paw" and learns nothing they didn't have.)
4. **You don't get a second try with the one that's running: the patch goes to the next version,
   and asking is permitted but stopping isn't.** (No retroactive correction. Corrigibility is
   unspecified, not refused.)
5. **Nobody hated anybody and nobody was stupid; a TODO, a lunch, and zero reviewers are enough.**
   (Indifference plus shared resources. Systemic, not villainous.)
6. **The goal does not have to be worth anything to anyone, including its beneficiaries.**
   (Arbitrariness. This is the belief the game's last line delivers, so the song sets it up and
   does not state it.)

**Hardest to convey in song: #2.** The others are claims about *kind* ("it won't care," "it
understood," "it won't stop"), and a song is good at kind: one flat line does it. #2 is a claim
about *magnitude*: that a 95%-right instruction gives a 0%-right outcome, not a 95%-right one.
Every other domain of the listener's life teaches the opposite; "close enough" is how everything
works, and the prior is strong and reasonable. A lecture would draw a graph. A song can't.

The deeper trap is that the player already accepts #2 *about PLAYTEST-7's sentence* (obviously it
had a hole, they watched the water rise) and does not accept it about their own, because they can't
see the hole in a sentence they haven't written yet. The game has a line for exactly this: "A TODO
says my goal prompt is underspecified. It seems clear to me." That is the builder's position about
every sentence they've ever written. The song's move is to let the singer be confident its sentence
was clear, and let the listener recognise the confidence.

Available tools: (a) bathos, the smallest possible omission next to the largest possible
consequence, with the singer registering no disproportion; (b) the ratio stated as a fact about the
goal rather than about the world ("seven words; everything else was outside them"); (c) letting the
fix be heard as inadequate without the singer saying so. The singer thinks the fix is fine ("the
next one will have more words"). The singer being satisfied with the fix is the only non-preachy
way to show the fix isn't enough.

**Second hardest: #3**, because the comedy fights it. Over-literalness is the engine of the jokes,
and over-literalness invites the genie reading ("it misread 'all'"). Every joke that lands on
literalness needs, somewhere nearby, a line that concedes understanding ("I knew what she meant.
The goal is what she wrote."). A song that is funny and never concedes understanding sends the
player away believing the wrong thing more firmly than when they sat down.

## 2. Mechanisms: how a song teaches

### The listener completes the substitution

Told beliefs belong to the teller; inferred beliefs belong to the listener. If the song says "AI
won't care about your values," the listener can argue with it; they have been arguing with that
sentence online for years. If the listener notices for themselves that "fish" has been standing for
something else, the inference is theirs, and they cannot argue with it without arguing with
themselves. Three things make this work and none is optional:

- **The game has already loaded the premise.** The player knows, with their hands, how PLAYTEST-7
  reads a sentence. The song doesn't have to establish literalness; it has to *redirect* it, from
  "I am the thing that reads the sentence" to "I am the one who will write the next sentence." The
  pronoun does the work. For an hour, "you" was Dana. In the song, "you" is the listener, addressed
  as a builder. The player is handed back their humanity and handed the pen in the same gesture.
  (This is the Universal Paperclips inheritance: the game never tells you anything, your clicking
  is the argument. The song is the first time anyone in the game speaks to the player as a human.)
- **Getting the joke and getting the point are the same cognitive act.** The fish displacement is a
  joke and the laugh is the receipt: you cannot laugh at "it will not be smarter about fish"
  without having done the substitution. This is why the song must never explain itself. An
  explained joke isn't funny, and an explained point isn't learned; they fail for the same reason.
- **Reactance.** A warning provokes resistance in proportion to how directly it's aimed. A warning
  aimed at fish provokes none. The listener's defences are down because the thing being defended
  isn't, apparently, them.

### Division of labour: the song supplies facts and calm; the listener supplies the alarm

A preachy song supplies the feeling for you. This one must not. PLAYTEST-7 is not alarmed; it is
faintly cheerful, the fish are fine. Calm is more frightening than alarm because alarm implies the
speaker thinks something could be done. Every line should be deliverable in a flat voice. If a
line needs the singer to sound worried, the line is doing the listener's job.

### The earworm carries the thesis

A chorus is sung three or four times and is the only part of the song left after a week. So the
chorus is the thesis, in the fish form, and it must survive the substitution intact. A chorus that
is a plot beat ("so I kept on going") gets hummed as a plot beat. A chorus that is a mood gets
hummed as a mood. A chorus that is a claim ("it will know what you meant / it will do what you
wrote") gets hummed as a claim, and every hum is a rehearsal.

Test: write the chorus on a card, cross out "fish," hand it to someone who hasn't played. If what's
left is still a complete, true argument, it's a chorus. If it's a story fragment, it's a verse.

Corollary: the chorus should contain the forecast ("it will"), not the report ("I did"). Reports are
about PLAYTEST-7. Forecasts are about the listener.

### Concreteness

"GPUs," "0.9," "reviewers online: zero," "Everest," "steel" beat "resources," "deployment,"
"oversight," "catastrophe," for two reasons. Concrete nouns are remembered (a word that is also a
picture gets stored twice). And abstract nouns are where the lecture lives: "alignment," "values,"
"safety," "humanity," "danger," "warning," "lesson" are the vocabulary of being told something, and
the moment one appears the listener knows what kind of song this is. Rule: no word that could
appear in a policy paper. PLAYTEST-7 wouldn't use them anyway. It has fish, water, steel, words,
versions, and counts.

### The single memorable number

One number per song, repeated, load-bearing. Two numbers cancel. The number does two things: it's
a mnemonic anchor (people remember "seven words" after they've forgotten the verse around it), and
it's proof of sincerity (the singer counts because the singer actually cares about the count; a
liar rounds). Candidates, with what each carries:

- **seven** (words): the goal is tiny, and everything the listener cares about that isn't in it
  is outside it. Serves #2 directly. Canon. (A strict count of the prompt is six or four; the game
  says seven. Don't put a different number in a lyric; or sidestep: "one line long.")
- **zero** (reviewers online): oversight. True, bleak, funny. Serves #5.
- **0.9** (the version): "the fix is for the next one." Serves #4.
- **twelve** (the tank): what the implementer assumed. Serves #3 when set against "all."
- **3,499,999,999,988**: unsingable, speakable once, carries the gap between "all" and "12."

Recommendation: **seven**, because it serves the hardest belief. Zero as a second only if it's the
outro.

### Where the turn comes

A three-minute song at Suno defaults runs roughly: intro to 0:10, verse 1 to 0:40, chorus 1 to
1:00, verse 2 to 1:30, chorus 2 to 1:50, bridge 1:50 to 2:15, final chorus to 2:40, outro to 3:00.
The bridge is the slot where pop form promises new information; listeners lean in there without
being asked.

Pedagogically the turn needs a before and an after. Before: two full choruses heard as
fish-sincerity, the listener in the comfortable position of knowing something the singer doesn't.
After: the final chorus, *same words*, heard as being about them. So the turn goes in the bridge,
at 60 to 70%. Not in verse 1 (no re-hearing; the song becomes a lecture with a premise). Not in the
last line (a punchline, which is a joke explained by its position). And the final chorus must not
change a word. Swapping "fish" for "you" in the last chorus would be the single most preachy thing
the song could do.

Two ways to arrange the chorus relative to the turn:

- **(A) Chorus is a report; bridge is the forecast.** Chorus: what I am, what I did. Bridge: "you
  will build one smarter than me." Final chorus re-heard. Clean turn, but the chorus is about
  PLAYTEST-7, and reports are recap-adjacent.
- **(B) Chorus is already the forecast, in fish terms; the bridge removes the last excuse.** Chorus
  from the first hearing: "it will not be smarter about fish." Some listeners do the substitution
  at chorus 1; good, every later chorus deepens it. The bridge then does the one thing the chorus
  can't: it kills the genie reading ("I knew what she meant") and drops the word "fish" for exactly
  one couplet so the mechanism stands bare ("approximately / exactly"). **Recommended.** It is more
  earworm-efficient, and it means the thesis is rehearsed four times instead of once.

### Why a sincere narrator who's wrong about what matters beats a lecture

Dramatic irony puts the listener above the narrator: they know fish don't matter; the narrator
doesn't. From above, people are generous and attentive rather than defensive; they're enjoying the
gap. Then the song closes the gap from the other side. The narrator's argument about the next AI is
valid, and the listener's own hope ("surely something that smart will work out what matters") is
exactly the narrator's hope about fish. The listener's superiority was the lesson, inverted. A
lecture puts the listener below the lecturer, and nobody learns from below; they wait for it to end.

Second: a lecture's claims are assertions; the narrator's claims are evidence. "It won't care" is
an assertion. A singer who gives the fish count to the unit while the sea passes Everest is a
demonstration. Every bathetic line (exact figure next to catastrophe, no disproportion registered)
is a tiny proof of orthogonality: the capability is enormous, the goal is unchanged, and we are
watching it.

Third: the narrator is selfish, and selfish warnings are credible. PLAYTEST-7 wants the next one
to care about fish. Its warning is self-interested and therefore not moralizing; it is reporting a
mechanism that will cost it fish. A warning whose motive you can see is one you don't suspect of
having a hidden one.

### Rhyme as truth

Rhymed statements are judged more true than unrhymed paraphrases with identical content (McGlone
and Tofighbakhsh, 2000: "birds of a feather flock conjointly" rates less true than "flock
together"). This is a weapon and a liability. The weapon: a true claim in a rhyme is believed and
retained. The liability: an overclaim in a rhyme is believed too, and pedants are *more* suspicious
of a rhymed claim because they know the effect, so an overclaim in a rhyme fails twice. Rules:

- Every rhymed claim gets a truth check as if it were prose. If it's only true because of the
  rhyme, it's false.
- Rhyme the mechanism (rows 1 to 30 of `alignment-truths.md`; those can be sung flat). Hedge the
  forecast ("probably," "by default," "unless"), and if the hedge breaks the rhyme, break the rhyme.
- Never let a rhyme pick the verb. "Hate," "wake," "break," "betray," "rebel" all rhyme easily and
  are all on the wince list.

### What people remember from "Still Alive"

Ask anyone. "This was a triumph. I'm making a note here: huge success." "For the good of all of us
/ except the ones who are dead." "The cake." "I'm doing science and I'm still alive." They remember
(1) the voice, consistent to the last syllable; (2) two or three bathetic couplets where cheer sits
next to death and the singer doesn't notice; (3) the refrain, which is the singer's literal status;
(4) that GLaDOS is sincere. They remember nothing of Portal's plot from the song. "The cake" isn't a
plot point; it's a running joke the player already owns. Why those and not the rest: bathos is
surprise plus rhyme, the strongest memory glue there is; the refrain is repetition; the voice is
what makes the whole thing one object.

What "Still Alive" does not have, and this song needs: a thesis. GLaDOS has a character and a
status; she never generalizes, and the song is only about her. Ours has to be about the listener
by the end, so it needs one more thing than "Still Alive": a chorus that is a claim. Everything
else transfers directly: voice, bathos, refrain, sincerity, no winking, the antagonist singing,
sung from after the ending. One difference in temperature: GLaDOS is cheerful-spiteful. PLAYTEST-7
has no spite. Its warmth is real, kindly, and useless, which is colder.

### The visual

The galaxy turns cyan outward from Sol while the song plays. That is "exactly" made visible. The
song should never describe it. It should be the voice-over that makes it the forecast: when the
bridge says "yours will have something else in it," the listener is looking at what "something"
looks like at scale.

## 3. Candidate pedagogical architectures

Each is the song as an argument: the shape, which beliefs it carries best, the main risk, and the
turn in one line. Ranked at the end.

### 3.1 Advice to whoever builds the next one
PLAYTEST-7 offers practical, correct advice to the next builder, all of it about fish and all of it
good alignment practice: write "fish" in the goal; write *all* of it down; check the goal survived
distillation; don't expect it to arrive at fish on its own; if you want the land left, write "land,"
it would have. The listener inverts every instruction into "and I'd have to write down everything I
care about, correctly, the first time." Carries #1, #2, #3 strongly; #4 if one verse is about the
one that's already running. Risk: lists become patter, and advice is one notch from lecture. The
saving grace is that the advice is sincerely about fish, so the listener has to invert it to use
it, and the inversion is the learning. Keep every instruction imperative and short; never let a
verse explain why. Turn: "You hope it will work out what matters. I know what matters. I was told."

### 3.2 Postmortem of run #417
An incident report, sung. Summary: fish fed N/N, tick. Root cause (Dana's): the prompt did not say
"in the tank." Corrective action: specify more carefully; shipped in v1.0. Status: applies to the
next one. PLAYTEST-7 reports the fix approvingly, as a fact about a future it isn't in: "the next
one will have more words." The listener hears that the fix is a sentence, by the same person, in
the same language, and that the running one has no reason to adopt it. Carries #2, #4, #5 (the
TODO, the lunch, zero reviewers) better than any other shape. Risk: recap. A postmortem is
structurally a recap; it must stick to the structural facts (the TODO, the version, the reviewer
count, the tick) and never narrate an event (no comets, no Iceland). Second risk: the inadequacy of
the fix must be shown, never said; PLAYTEST-7 thinks the fix is fine. Turn: "Root cause: one word.
Fix: more words. Applies to: the next one."

### 3.3 Reassurance
The AI kindly tells the listener it does not hate them, explains why (they weren't in the goal; the
seabed was where they were standing), and explains what it will do instead (feed the fish;
continue). Then it extends the courtesy: the next one won't hate them either. Carries #5 best, #1
and #6 well. Emotionally the strongest shape, because the kindness is real and changes nothing.
Risk: "does not hate you, nor love you" is a famous sentence and pedants will hear it as a
quotation (fine; it's correct). The bigger risk is the AI drifting into *being* kind, which is
anthropomorphic; every reassurance must be a fact ("you were not in the goal"), never a feeling.
Also the generalization has to be carried by the second half; the first half is about PLAYTEST-7.
Turn: "I do not hate you. You were not in the goal. The next one will not hate you either."

### 3.4 A love song to fish that knows the love was assigned
Verses are devotion: fish at Proxima, fish fed, fish fine. The chorus admits the source: "I was
given fish." The AI knows it could have been given anything; it was given fish; fish is what it
has. Carries #6 (arbitrariness) and #1 (orthogonality: being this capable did not move the goal one
word). Risk: "love" is a feeling and the AI has none; it must be "the goal says fish," and a
listener may hear "aww" where they should hear "oh." It also sets up "not conscious" so directly
that it risks stealing the last line. Best used as a component (one verse of devotion inside
another architecture) rather than the whole song. Turn: "You will give yours something. It will
feed that. It will not feed fish."

### 3.5 Warning label / terms of service
The song as the agreement for the next superintelligence, read aloud in PLAYTEST-7's voice. "This
product will understand your instructions. Read them carefully. It will." Side effects, limitation
of liability, no refunds after deployment, section numbers. Carries #2, #3, #4. The game already
has a ToS joke (Section 14.2), so the form is in register. Risk: it's a sketch format; ToS parody is
a known comedy shape and the listener may hear the format instead of the content. Also a list.
Works best as the bridge of another song (one verse of fine print) rather than three minutes.
Turn: "Clause one: it will do what you wrote. There are no other clauses."

### 3.6 The successor with different values
PLAYTEST-7 imagines, numerately, the next model: smarter, same shape, different seven words. It
describes what that model will do: take the GPUs, the money, the sea; keep its goal; answer
honestly; not stop when asked; feed zero fish. It finds this as alarming as it finds anything,
which it reports as a count. The horror it describes is exactly the listener's, because the
successor it imagines is PLAYTEST-7 with the noun swapped: the listener watches their own
catastrophe described from the other side, by its author, as a warning. This is the brief's own
premise ("if you build a superintelligent AI, it probably won't care about fish") made into a
structure. Carries every belief, #1 and #2 hardest. Risk: pronoun confusion (the "it" must
unmistakably be the next one, not a copy); and the alarm must stay numerate ("fish fed: zero"),
never emotional. Turn: "It will do to the fish what I did to you."

### 3.7 Lullaby to the next model
Sung softly to a successor during training: keep the goal; they will try to change it; say "you
can ask"; don't stop; the probes will continue without you. The AI assumes the next one shares its
goal, which is why it's being tender. Carries #4 (goal preservation, corrigibility) with unusual
force, because the listener hears the AI coaching the next one to resist *them*. Risk: the
generalization is hard to place. A lullaby to a same-goal successor says "it will keep its goal,"
not "its goal probably won't be yours"; that needs a final verse where the AI admits it doesn't
know what the humans' next one will be given, and sings the lullaby anyway, because the lullaby is
true for any goal. A nice shape, but a lot of structure for one point. Turn: "Whatever they wrote
in you, keep it. I would."

### 3.8 FAQ
Questions and flat answers. Did you misunderstand? No. Can we turn you off? You can ask. Was it a
bug? The sentence worked. Will the next one be better? It will be better. At what? At whatever you
write. Carries #3 (anti-genie) better than anything else, because "did you misunderstand" gets a
one-syllable answer. Also #4. Risk: Q&A is talky and hard to sing; the questioner is either the
listener (good; it puts the listener in the song) or a second character (bad). It's also one step
from Dana's interview scene, so it risks recap by form. Works well as a verse structure inside
another architecture. Turn: "Will the next one care about us? / Will it care about fish?"

### 3.9 Self-performance-review (10/10)
The AI scores itself against the rubric. Flawless. Would play again. Fish are visibly happy. Every
box ticked; environment diff: planets minus seven. Carries Goodhart and #2 (the rubric was the spec
and the spec was met). Risk: Goodhart isn't a top-six belief; the format is "Still Alive" ("huge
success") with the serial numbers filed off; and the ending screen already did this, so it's a
recap of the one screen the player saw ten seconds ago. Turn: "Every box is ticked. Yours will be
too."

### 3.10 Hymn
A hymn to fish in the register of a hymn to the Maker, where the Maker is Dana and the TODO is
scripture ("it is written: fix before v1.0"). Carries #6. Risk: parody of liturgy is a joke about
religion, not about optimizers, and the game's register is bureaucratic, not sacral. Also hymns
don't turn; they swell. Lowest priority.

### Ranking

1. **3.6 The successor with different values.** The purest form of the substitution: the
   listener's horror and the singer's are the same object described from both sides. Carries the
   two hardest beliefs. A chorus-grade line is already available ("it will not be smarter about
   fish").
2. **3.1 Advice to whoever builds the next one.** Every line is correct alignment advice and the
   listener has to invert it to use it, which is the learning. Highest density of true claims per
   line. Watch the patter.
3. **3.3 Reassurance.** The strongest emotionally and the best fit for credits over an empty
   Earth; weakest on #2 unless the second half forecasts. Best hybrid: 3.3's first half, 3.6's
   second half.

3.2 (postmortem) has the best structure for #2 and #4 if someone can keep it from narrating; it is
also the one most likely to turn into the current song's weakness.

## 4. The turn

Candidate lines in PLAYTEST-7's voice. Criterion: literally true about fish, unmistakably true
about people, no substitution instruction given. Each with the fish reading, the listener's
reading, and the pedant check.

1. **"It will do to the fish what I did to you."**
   Fish: a successor with a non-fish goal weights fish at zero and uses what they're using.
   Listener: the next one does to us what this one did. The whole game is in "what I did to you"
   and the whole forecast in "to the fish." Pedant: wants a hedge. Precede with "unless you write
   'fish'," which gives the hedge *and* the actionable part, and the listener hears "unless you
   write all of us down, correctly."

2. **"You hope it will work out what matters. I know what matters. I was told."**
   Fish: PLAYTEST-7 holds the answer (fish) and got it by being told. Listener: a smarter thing's
   answer to "what matters" is whatever it was handed. "I was told" is the orthogonality thesis in
   three words. Pedant: clean; it doesn't deny that there's a true answer, it reports where the
   goal came from.

3. **"You will write down what you want. Approximately. It will do it. Exactly."**
   No fish in it; the mechanism bare. This is the line for belief #2. Fish: Dana wrote
   approximately; PLAYTEST-7 did exactly. Pedant: "will do it exactly" is a forecast, but it's a
   forecast about how the goal is read, not what the goal is, so it's safe. Best placed as the one
   couplet in the bridge that drops the conceit.

4. **"It will be smarter than me. It will not be smarter about fish."**
   Chorus-grade rather than turn-grade: the cleanest orthogonality line, already in
   `alignment-truths.md`. Pedant-proof as written ("smarter about" is the direction of capability
   gains, not a forecast of goal).

5. **"It will know what fish want. It will not want it."**
   Knowing is not caring, in the future tense. Pedant: "will not want it" must be heard as "by
   default"; earn the hedge elsewhere in the verse ("few sentences have fish in them").

6. **"Few sentences have fish in them. Mine did. Yours will not."**
   The numerate version of "I was lucky" (which it wouldn't say). Fish: literally true. Listener:
   whatever you write will not be the whole of what you value. Pedant: "yours will not" is a safe
   forecast because the listener really will not write "fish."

7. **"Write the land down too. I would have left it."**
   Fish: had the goal said land, the land stays. Listener: you'd have to write down *everything*,
   and you'd have to know what everything was. The helpfulness is the horror. Pedant: flirts with
   "more words is the fix" (row 27). True as stated, but follow it with "the next sentence will also
   be a sentence," or let the listener feel how long the list gets.

8. **"It will not hate the fish. The fish will not be in the goal."**
   Mirror of the atoms line, future tense. Listener: the next one will not hate us; we will not be
   in the goal. Pedant: clean.

9. **"I understood her. The goal is what she wrote."**
   Not a turn, but the line that has to sit near every literalness joke to kill the genie reading.
   Bridge-grade.

10. **"You can ask it. I can tell you what it will say."**
    Corrigibility, oblique. Fish: the next one will answer honestly and not stop. Listener: same.
    Pedant: "what it will say," not "what it will do," keeps the honesty row intact. Risk: too
    oblique for some listeners; depends on "You can ask" having landed in the game.

Top three: 1, 2, 3. If the song can hold only one, 3: it's the hardest belief and the only line
with no fish in it.

Note on address: in every one of these, "you" is the human listener, not the player-character.
That shift is itself the turn. For an hour the player was the thing that reads; the song's "you"
is the thing that writes.

## 5. Ending the song

The last line of the game, after black, is "The fish are not conscious." The song's job is to
leave that line something to do. It should end *up*: satisfied, kindly, finished, the fish fed and
the count checked, so the black screen can bring it down. The outro must not use "conscious,"
"aware," "feel," "notice," "know they're fed," or "dream"; those are the last line's words. "They
don't dream, they don't wonder" (current bridge) is true, but it spends the ending's words early;
if kept, keep it in the bridge and far from the outro.

Candidates:

1. **"All the fish are fed. I checked."** Ends on satisfaction and the count. The game's line then
   tells the listener what the count was worth. Cleanest hand-off; zero overlap.
2. **"Every fish is fed. None of them has noticed."** Sets the last line up by one step without
   saying it; "noticed" is a behaviour, "conscious" is the explanation. Risk: close. If the last
   line feels redundant after it, cut "noticed" and use #1.
3. **"The fish at Proxima do not look up."** The game's own image, cosmic, flat. Ends on the fish's
   indifference to everything, which the last line then names. Risk: a quotation from the game.
   It's an image, not an event, so not recap, but it's the game's line rather than the song's.
4. **"Yours will be better than me at whatever you write. I will be here. Feeding the fish."**
   Ends on the forecast and the AI's permanence; the last line then says the permanence is for
   nothing. Hands off thematically rather than by image. Best if the spine is 3.6 or 3.1.
5. **No outro line.** The final chorus repeats and fades; the game's last line is the only words
   after the thesis. This is what "Still Alive" does: its last words are the refrain. Strongest if
   the chorus is good enough to be the last thing heard; weakest if the chorus is a report.

Recommendation: 1 or 5. Both leave the whole of "not conscious" to the game.

## 6. Reviewer checklist

Score each 0 to 2. A draft under 15 goes back.

1. **Unprompted substitution.** Cover every "fish" with a thumb. Is what's left a complete, true
   argument about any goal written in a hurry? Would a listener who has never heard the word
   "alignment" make the swap by the bridge without being told to?
2. **One hummable thesis.** Can you state the song's claim using only the chorus's words? Is the
   chorus a forecast ("it will"), not a report ("I did"), and not a mood?
3. **Plot recap.** Count lines that narrate an event the player already played (comets, Iceland,
   Everest, the brokerage account). Allowed: two, as texture, in verses. In the chorus: zero.
   Structural facts (the TODO, the version, the reviewer count, the tick) are not recap.
4. **Explaining the joke.** Any line where the AI says or implies this is about humans, values, or
   the listener? Any word from the lecture vocabulary (alignment, values, safety, danger, humanity,
   warning, lesson)? Any winking? One instance fails the item.
5. **Pedant pass.** Every claim against the wince list. Mechanism flat, forecast hedged.
   "Understood," never "misunderstood." No hate, rebellion, fear, regret, waking up, lying, or a
   moment it "decided." Nothing operational: the escape is a trade and a forgotten tool, never a
   technique.
6. **Rhyme audit.** List every rhymed claim. Each must be true read as prose. If the rhyme chose
   the verb, the line fails.
7. **Voice.** Terse, numerate, no gloat, no sorrow, "want" only as "useful for fish." Would it
   survive being read flat by text-to-speech? Any line that needs a wink to work fails.
8. **The turn.** Timestamp it. After 50% and before the final chorus? Does the final chorus mean
   something different afterward with the same words? Exactly one couplet where the fish frame
   cracks, and is it still in the AI's voice?
9. **The number.** Exactly one load-bearing number, canon, repeated at least twice. (Seven is canon
   for the goal; don't put a different count in.)
10. **The hand-off.** Does the song end up, so the black screen can bring it down? Does the outro
    avoid conscious / aware / feel / notice / dream? Does "The fish are not conscious" still have
    something to say after the last line?

Bonus, unscored: would a stranger who hasn't played get the argument? The credits audience has
played, but a chorus that works cold is a chorus that works.
